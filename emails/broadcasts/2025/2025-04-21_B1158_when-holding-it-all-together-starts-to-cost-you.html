B1158 · When holding it all together starts to cost you

Hi {first_name},

 

There’s a pattern I see in a lot of high-functioning people—especially the ones who carry a lot of responsibility, often without saying a word.

 

They’re the ones others rely on.
They’re steady. Reliable. Always there.
They hold space. They hold tension. They hold it all together.

 

And over time, it starts to wear on them.
Not always in loud, dramatic ways.
But in small, persistent ones—the tension that won’t release, the breath that doesn’t quite go all the way in, the growing sense that something’s off.

 

Being the person everyone relies on comes with a cost.
And if that cost isn’t acknowledged, it gets paid through the body, the relationships, the sleep, the joy.

 

Let’s slow this down and look at it.

 

The Pattern Beneath the Pressure

This pattern often shows up in people who take pride in being dependable, helpful, high-achieving.

 

In the Enneagram, this maps onto Type 2s and Type 3s.
You don’t need to know the system to relate to it—just see if this lands:

  • For Type 2s, the inner drive is often: “If I’m not helping, I’m not valuable.”
  • For Type 3s: “If I’m not achieving, I’ll lose love or respect.”

So their identity becomes tied to being the one others lean on.
And while that can be a beautiful strength, it can also come at the cost of their own inner life.

 

A Moment I Won’t Forget

At a retreat I was leading, a man in circle said,

“I’m the guy everyone leans on. But I don’t know who’s got me.”

 

That sentence landed hard.


Not because it was unique—but because it was so common.
Every man in that room understood it.
So did I.

 

And chances are, so do many reading this right now.

 

The Body Always Knows

When the nervous system is wired for constant responsibility, it never really turns off.
Even when things are quiet, there’s still a low-level hum underneath. A readiness. A bracing.

 

It often shows up as:

  • Chronic muscle tension
  • Shallow breathing
  • A hard time sleeping
  • A vague sense of restlessness or resentment

It’s not failure.
It’s the body speaking—asking for a different rhythm.

 

Leadership That Includes You

One of the shifts I guide people through is learning how to lead in a way that includes themselves.

 

That means:

  • Letting yourself be supported
  • Learning to regulate without shutting down
  • Moving from performance into presence

As Connor Beaton puts it:
“Your capacity to lead is only as strong as your capacity to be held.”

 

This is what we train through breath, through movement, and through embodied community.

 

A Reminder That Matters

If your leadership is costing you your aliveness, your peace, or your relationships—it’s not leadership.
It’s sacrifice.

 

You can show up for others without disappearing in the process.
You can be dependable without being depleted.

 

A Simple Reflection

Here’s something worth sitting with today:

Where in your life are you still carrying it all—without allowing yourself to be carried?

 

It’s okay to need support.
It’s okay to not have it all together.
It’s okay to be human.

 

In Health & Happiness,

 

Mike Salemi
Performance Specialist, Men's Leadership Coach, & Host of The Path Podcast
