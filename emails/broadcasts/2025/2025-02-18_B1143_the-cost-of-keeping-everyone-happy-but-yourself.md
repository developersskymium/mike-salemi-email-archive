# the cost of keeping everyone happy but yourself

Hey {first_name},

 

A man inside The Vital Man Collective recently said something that I deeply resonated with from my own journey in prior years:

 

"I do everything I can to be a good man. I show up. I work hard. I try to make the people in my life happy. But no matter how much I give, it never feels like enough."

 

Maybe you or a man you know has felt that, too.

 

You pride yourself on being solid, someone people can count on. You handle responsibilities. You don’t cause problems. You try to be the guy who makes things easier for everyone else.

 

And yet, at some point, you start to notice the cost.

 

You hold back what you really think because you don’t want to create conflict.


You suppress your needs because you tell yourself they aren’t as important.


You carry an unspoken resentment because you give, and give, and give… but it never seems to come back to you in the way you hope.

 

This is the weight of Nice Guy Syndrome.

 

And it has nothing to do with kindness.

 

The Truth About ‘Nice Guys’

A lot of men confuse being a good man with being a man who shrinks himself to avoid discomfort.

 

They think:

“If I keep people happy, they’ll appreciate me.”
“If I avoid conflict, things will go smoothly.”
“If I put others first, eventually things will balance out.”

 

But here’s what actually happens:

People don’t respect a man who constantly abandons himself.
Avoiding conflict doesn’t make it disappear—it just shows up in other ways.
The longer you suppress your own needs, the harder it becomes to even recognize what they are.

 

John Wineland talks about the false virtue of niceness—how so many men wear it like a badge of honor, but in reality, it’s a cover for fear.

 

The fear of being too much.
The fear of being seen as selfish.
The fear of being rejected for standing firm.

 

And so instead, these men become agreeable, accommodating, and easy to be around.

 

Not because it’s who they truly are.
But because they believe it’s what will make them acceptable.

 

And yet—deep down—they feel the cost of it.

 

They know they’re holding back.
They know they’ve made themselves smaller.
They know they’ve betrayed themselves in small, subtle ways over time.

 

A man can only do this for so long before he starts to break under the weight of it.

 

What Happens When a Man Reclaims His Power?

 

The moment a man stops living for other people’s approval, something shifts.

 

He stops sugarcoating his words and starts speaking clearly and directly.
He stops explaining himself when he sets a boundary.
He stops overextending himself in relationships that only drain him.

 

And ironically?

 

That’s the moment people actually start respecting him.

 

Because people don’t trust a man who betrays himself.

 

A Challenge for You

For the next 24 hours, I want you to pay attention to the moments when you feel the urge to soften your words, backtrack, or accommodate someone else at your own expense.

 

Where do you hold back your truth?
Where do you avoid discomfort?
Where do you downplay your needs, even when you feel them deeply?

 

And then—just once—choose a different response.

 

Say what you actually mean.
Hold your boundary without apologizing for it.
Let someone feel uncomfortable with your truth, and stand firm in it.

 

At first, it might feel unnatural.

 

But if you sit with it, you’ll feel something stronger beneath the discomfort:

Your own power.

 

With respect,

  

Mike Salemi
Men's Coach & Host of The Path Podcast
